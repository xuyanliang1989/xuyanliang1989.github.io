## What to look for

- **Steel type and heat treat.** At this price you're mostly looking at 8Cr13MoV/8Cr14MoV, 420HC, AUS-8, and the occasional D2 or 14C28N. A well-treated budget steel beats a poorly treated premium one every time. Don't chase the steel name; chase the brand's reputation for heat treat.
- **Handle ergonomics and grip.** A fixed blade transfers force straight into your palm. Look for contoured scales, a finger guard or choil, and a handle long enough for your hand with gloves on. Slick, blocky handles cause hot spots fast.
- **Sheath quality.** A great knife in a bad sheath is a bad knife. You want positive retention, a belt loop or clip that actually holds, and a design that lets you re-sheathe one-handed without looking.
- **Blade geometry for your use.** A full flat grind slices well and is easy to sharpen; a scandi grind is great for woodwork; a thick saber grind is tough but a poor slicer. Match the grind to what you'll actually do — food prep, kindling, or both.

## FAQ

**Is a fixed-blade knife legal to carry while camping in the US?**
Legality varies by state and even by county, and many states restrict blade length or carry method. Check your destination's rules before you go — a 3.5-inch blade like the Rat-3 or Pendleton is legal in far more places than a 6-inch Buck 119.

**Carbon steel or stainless for a camping knife?**
Carbon steel (1095, Cro-Van) takes a keener edge and is easier to sharpen in the field but rusts without care. Stainless (420HC, 12C27, 4116) resists corrosion and is better for wet, salty, or coastal trips. For most casual campers, stainless is the lower-maintenance choice.

**Can a $20 knife really hold up?**
Yes — the Morakniv Companion is proof. The catch is usually the sheath and handle rather than the blade. Budget knives from reputable brands with good heat treat will outcut and outlast no-name knives at twice the price.

**Do I need a full-tang knife?**
Not necessarily. Full-tang construction (like the BK2) is tougher for batoning and prying, but many excellent knives use a stick or partial tang and are perfectly strong for normal camp tasks. Match the construction to how hard you'll actually use it.

**How do I keep a carbon steel blade from rusting at camp?**
Wipe it dry after every use, keep a thin coat of oil (mineral oil or a dedicated knife oil) on the blade, and never store it wet in its sheath. A small oil-soaked cloth in your kit makes field maintenance a ten-second job.
