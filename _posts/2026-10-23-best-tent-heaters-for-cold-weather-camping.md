It's 2 a.m. in late October, the temperature has dropped to 24°F, and the inside of your tent is fogging up with every breath. Your sleeping bag is rated for the cold, but your face is freezing, your water bottle has a skin of ice, and you're lying there doing math about how many hours until sunrise. That's the moment most cold-weather campers start thinking seriously about a tent heater — and also the moment they realize that heating a tent safely is a different problem than heating a garage or a cabin. A tent is a small, sealed, fabric space, and the wrong heater in that space is genuinely dangerous. This guide walks through the heaters that actually work for cold-weather camping, what to look for before you buy, and how to run one without turning your tent into a hazard.

## What to look for

- **Ventilation and combustion type.** Any propane or fuel-burning heater produces carbon monoxide. Look for heaters with a low-oxygen shutoff (ODS) sensor, and plan to crack a vent regardless. Electric heaters sidestep this entirely if you have shore power.
- **BTU output vs. tent size.** A three-season two-person tent needs far less heat than a big cabin tent. Oversized heaters cycle on and off constantly and create condensation; undersized ones run all night and never catch up.
- **Safety features.** Tip-over shutoff, automatic low-oxygen shutoff, and a stable base matter more in a tent than anywhere else. A heater that can be knocked over by a sleeping bag in the dark is a bad choice.
- **Noise and fuel/runtime.** Catalytic and radiant heaters run silent; forced-air and electric heaters add fan noise. Check runtime per fuel canister or tank, and check power draw if you're on a battery station.

## FAQ

**Can I leave a propane tent heater running all night?**
Most manufacturers, including Mr. Heater, advise against sleeping with a propane heater running unattended, and it's a real carbon monoxide risk in a sealed tent. The safer pattern is to warm the tent before bed, shut the heater off, and rely on a properly rated sleeping bag overnight — or use an electric heater on a powered site if you want heat all night.

**How many BTU do I need to heat a tent?**
A rough rule is about 1,000 BTU per 10 square feet of tent floor in mild cold, and more in hard freezes, but tent insulation is essentially zero so the numbers are loose. A 4,000 BTU low setting handles a small two-person tent, while large cabin tents often need 9,000–18,000 BTU to feel a difference.

**Is a catalytic or radiant heater better for a tent?**
Radiant heaters warm objects and people directly, which suits tents where you're sitting in one spot. Catalytic heaters produce more gentle, widespread warmth but cost more and still produce carbon monoxide, so ventilation and an ODS sensor matter either way.

**Do I still need to vent the tent with a low-oxygen shutoff heater?**
Yes. An ODS sensor is a backup, not a substitute for ventilation — crack a vent or leave a window partly open regardless. Condensation is the other reason: propane combustion releases water vapor, and a sealed tent will be soaking wet by morning.

**Can I run a tent heater off a portable power station?**
Only for very short periods with most units. A 1,500-watt electric heater will drain a typical 500–1,000 Wh power station in well under an hour at full output, so electric heat really needs shore power or a large, expensive battery setup to last a night.
