## How to Think About Your First Tent

Before we get into specific models, it helps to understand what separates a tent that will make you love camping from one that will make you swear it off. Beginners tend to over-index on price and under-index on three things: the rainfly, the floor, and the pole setup.

**The rainfly** is the separate layer that goes over the top of the tent. A full-coverage rainfly that comes down almost to the ground is the single biggest factor in staying dry. Many cheap tents have a tiny "vestibule-only" fly that leaves the sides exposed — that's what leaks in wind-driven rain.

**The floor** matters almost as much. Look for a bathtub-style floor (where the waterproof material curves up the sides a few inches) and a hydrostatic head rating. Anything under 1,500mm is a gamble in real rain. A footprint or tarp underneath adds years of life.

**Poles** come in two main flavors: fiberglass (cheap, heavier, prone to splintering) and aluminum (lighter, stronger, more expensive). For a beginner on a budget, fiberglass is fine for car camping. If you plan to backpack, aluminum is worth the upgrade.

**Capacity ratings** are optimistic. A "2-person" tent comfortably fits one adult with gear; a "4-person" fits two adults and a dog. Size up if you're unsure.

## FAQ

**How much should a beginner spend on a first tent?**
Most beginners are well served by spending between $100 and $220. That range gets you a tent with a full-coverage rainfly, a waterproof floor, and poles that won't fail on the second trip. Spending less than $70 usually means compromising on weather protection, and spending more than $300 is hard to justify until you know you'll camp regularly.

**What size tent should a beginner buy?**
Size up from the stated capacity. A "2-person" tent comfortably fits one adult plus gear, and a "4-person" fits two adults and a dog. If you're camping with a partner, a 3-person tent gives you breathing room; if you're solo, a 2-person tent is a comfortable choice that still packs small.

**Do I need a footprint or ground tarp?**
A footprint isn't strictly required, but it's one of the cheapest ways to extend your tent's life. It protects the floor from rocks, roots, and abrasion, and it keeps moisture from wicking up through the bottom. Many tents, including the Kelty Discovery Trail, include one; if yours doesn't, a cut-to-size tarp works fine.

**Is a 3-season tent enough for beginners?**
Yes. A 3-season tent is designed for spring, summer, and fall camping, which covers almost all beginner trips. Four-season tents are heavier, more expensive, and built for snow and high wind — you don't need one unless you're planning winter camping, which is a different skill set entirely.

**How do I keep my tent dry in the rain?**
Three things matter most: pick a tent with a full-coverage rainfly, seal the seams with a seam sealer if the manufacturer recommends it, and pitch on high ground so water doesn't pool underneath. Also, avoid touching the tent walls from inside during rain — contact with the fly can wick water through. If you do all that, you'll stay dry through most ordinary storms.
