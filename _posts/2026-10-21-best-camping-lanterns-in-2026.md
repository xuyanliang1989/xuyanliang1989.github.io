## What to look for

- **Lumens and dimming range:** You want a high end for cooking and camp chores (300–800 lumens is plenty for a family site) and a genuinely low end for reading or midnight bathroom runs without destroying your night vision. Look for stepless dimming, not just three preset levels.
- **Power source and runtime:** Rechargeable lithium batteries are now the norm, but check for USB-C charging and pass-through use so the lantern works while plugged into a power bank. If you camp off-grid for days, a model that also runs on AA batteries is a smart hedge.
- **Weather sealing:** An IPX4 rating handles rain and splashes; IPX6 or better survives a dropped-in-the-creek moment. The globe and switch are the usual failure points, so pay attention to how the housing is built, not just the electronics.
- **Collapsibility and hangability:** A lantern that packs small and has a real hanging loop or built-in hook is far more useful than a bulky one. Check the folded size and weight if you're backpacking.

## FAQ

**How many lumens do I actually need for camping?**
For a family campsite, 300–500 lumens is plenty for cooking and hanging out, and you'll mostly run it lower than that. A genuinely low, dimmable setting matters more than a high maximum, since blinding light ruins night vision and annoys your campmates.

**Are rechargeable lanterns better than battery-powered ones?**
Rechargeable lanterns are more convenient and cheaper to run over time, and USB-C charging is now standard. That said, a battery-powered backup like the UCO Clarus 2 is smart for emergency kits and long off-grid trips where you can't recharge.

**Can I use a camping lantern as a power bank?**
Some models, including the Black Diamond Moji Charging Station and BioLite AlpenGlow 500, include a power-bank function. It's usually enough to top off a phone or headlamp overnight, but check the battery capacity if you plan to rely on it for more than that.

**What IP rating should a camping lantern have?**
Look for at least IPX4 for rain and splashes, and IPX6 or better if you camp in wet conditions or near water. The globe and switch are the usual failure points, so a well-sealed housing matters as much as the rating itself.

**How do I hang a lantern in a tent without a hook?**
Many lanterns, including the Goal Zero Lighthouse Mini and Nitecore LR70, have folding hooks or legs that convert into hanging points. A small carabiner or a loop of cord around the tent apex also works, and magnetic bases like the LR70's can stick to a vehicle or metal pole.
