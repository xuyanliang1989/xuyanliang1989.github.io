It's 6:40 a.m., it's 34 degrees, and your hands are shaking as you try to light the one thing standing between you and hot coffee. The wind has been gusting all night, your lighter is cold-soaked, and the stove you grabbed at the last minute is sputtering like it's mad at you. Meanwhile, your buddy's canister stove is already roaring and his water is rolling. That gap — between a stove that works and one that fights you — is what separates a good morning from a miserable one on the trail. A backpacking stove is one of the lightest pieces of gear you'll carry and one of the most consequential. Get it right and every trip starts with a hot meal. Get it wrong and you'll be eating cold soaked oats in the dark.

## What to look for

- **Weight (with fuel):** The stove body is only part of the number. A 2-ounce stove that needs a heavy canister and a separate windscreen can end up heavier than a 4-ounce integrated system. Weigh the whole kit.
- **Fuel type:** Canister stoves are the easiest to use and the most popular for three-season backpacking. Liquid-fuel (white gas) stoves win in deep cold and at altitude. Alcohol stoves are the lightest but slowest and fussiest.
- **Wind and cold performance:** A stove that can't handle a breeze will waste fuel and time. Look for built-in wind protection or plan to carry a screen. Cold canisters lose pressure below freezing.
- **Boil time and fuel efficiency:** A fast boil saves fuel, which saves weight over a multi-day trip. Check published boil times but treat them as best-case.
- **Simmer control:** If you actually cook (not just boil water), a stove with a real valve and a wide flame spread matters more than a tenth of an ounce.

## FAQ

**Can I use a canister stove below freezing?**
Canister stoves lose pressure as temperatures drop, and below about freezing performance falls off sharply. You can help by keeping the canister warm in a pocket or using a stove with a regulator, but for sustained sub-freezing trips a liquid-fuel stove is the more reliable choice.

**How much fuel do I need for a multi-day trip?**
A rough rule of thumb is about 0.5 to 1 ounce of fuel per person per day for boiling water, depending on wind and temperature. A small canister typically covers a solo weekend, while longer trips or group cooking call for a larger canister or a refillable system.

**Is an integrated stove like the Jetboil worth the extra weight?**
If you mostly boil water and value speed and convenience, yes — the efficiency and integrated design often offset the extra weight over a trip. If you cook real meals or count every gram, a bare canister stove is more flexible.

**Do I need a windscreen with a canister stove?**
Most bare canister stoves benefit from one, and some manufacturers warn against enclosing the canister too tightly because of heat buildup. Use a screen that shields the flame without trapping heat around the fuel canister, and never use one inside a tent.

**What's the difference between canister and liquid-fuel stoves for backpacking?**
Canister stoves are lighter, simpler, and easier to light, which suits most three-season backpacking. Liquid-fuel stoves are heavier and fussier but perform far better in cold and at altitude, and they can run on multiple fuel types when availability is uncertain.
